1. Description of the Application

Forkcast is a dish-level rating, review, and recommendation platform for Notre Dame dining halls. Students currently lack a centralized online platform to share their dining preferences and feedback. Dining halls rely on traditional methods, such as whiteboards, to gather student input. Our system aims to make this feedback more accessible and help students discover meals they enjoy.

Our system will obtain daily dining hall menus from dining.nd.edu and allow students to rate individual dishes. We are considering several rating formats, including a five-star scale, like/dislike buttons, or three recommendation levels: "Don't recommend," "Recommend," and "Strongly recommend." We also plan to implement a chat section that allows students to write comments about the food and provide additional, more substantial feedback. Students can also recommend dishes they would like the dining hall to serve in the future, and others can upvote or downvote these suggestions using a Reddit-style voting system, helping the dining staff identify the most popular requests.

Our project seeks to encourage student participation as well as recommend dishes that a user might enjoy. By taking advantage of the rotating dining hall menu, students can be notified when their favorites return. Furthermore, based on a student's previous ratings, dishes that they may enjoy can be highlighted. This gives students a practical benefit for sharing their preferences and helps them plan where and what to eat.

Ratings and comments are attached to the dish itself, so a student's opinion builds up over time instead of resetting each day. Separately, every time a student eats a dish they can tap a button recording how that serving compared to usual: better than usual, as usual, or worse than usual. Those taps accumulate into a running history, so the dish page can show whether it has been good lately without losing the student's overall rating. Collecting student ratings gives the dining hall direct feedback on their offerings and helps students discover which dishes their peers enjoy.

Our system does not stop there. It aims to use more sophisticated recommendation algorithms. In our current design, students participate in an optional survey about their food preferences during sign-up, which builds a preliminary preference profile for users. We then build a correlation matrix between different dishes once we have enough data, and update the user's preference profile based on their ratings and potentially their comments. Based on the volume of data, we can incorporate historical ratings into the algorithm.

As a separate feature, we are considering a "find a dining partner" tab that allows people to look for dining partners and match people with similar taste preferences, to augment the social aspect of our system.

Eventually, we wish to expand the app to other campus restaurants and beyond campuses.

2. Usefulness

Our application has both a personal and a social aspect.

For the personal aspect, it helps students make more informed decisions about where and what to eat. By combining daily menus, student reviews, and personalized recommendations, our system helps students discover dishes that match their preferences and have better dining experiences, ultimately improving the dining quality at Notre Dame. It also gives students a practical reason to rate: the more a student rates, the better their recommendations get.

For the social aspect, students can learn about peer preferences, exchange their opinions on dishes, and find dining partners, getting to know more people on campus through perhaps the most ancient and most enjoyable way to know people: eating together. The system will also provide dining hall staff with a centralized source of student feedback. Aggregated ratings and comments could help staff identify popular dishes, recurring concerns, and changes in student preferences.

Similar and equivalent applications

Several services offer related functionality.

  • The Notre Dame Dining website provides access to dining hall menus, but it does not allow students to rate the individual dishes, which is what our system is trying to solve.
  • Eat UNC, a similar project at the University of North Carolina, offers dish ratings, saved favorites, and notifications when favorite dishes return. However, our system aims to use more sophisticated notifications and food recommendation algorithms instead of just notifying for starred dishes.
  • There are few dish-based rating food apps on the market. Some small ones include Plateful, which lets users rate dishes and offers recommendations based on personal taste profiles. Our system is different in that it helps students choose from today's dining hall offerings, with feedback about today's preparations. Moreover, through the community at Notre Dame, we hope our app can provide a feedback channel for the Notre Dame dining staff, both through the ratings and the dishes students propose.

3. Realness

Our data comes from two places: the Notre Dame dining website, and the students who use the system.

Dining options

We source our dining data from the Notre Dame dining website at nd.nutrislice.com. This data can be obtained by scraping the site, and it gives us:

  • Dish name
  • Dining location
  • Meal periods
  • Dates

One caveat is that the nutrition information takes the form of a nutrition label, so the data will have to be parsed from it.

Student-generated data

  • Sign-up food preferences
  • Ratings
  • Comments
  • Dish suggestions
  • Upvotes and downvotes

The dining options side is real, public, and daily. The student-generated side starts empty by definition. Depending on how much data we need for the project, we may simulate some of it, and we will ask the professor about our data needs before doing so.

4. Functionality

4.1 Basic Functions

Our application will provide the following core functionality.

Browse dining halls and menus
Users can select a Notre Dame dining hall and view the dishes being served for a particular date and meal period. Dishes will be organized into categories to make the menu easy to navigate.
Search for dishes
Users can search for a specific dish and view information about where and when it is being served, along with its ratings and reviews.
Rate dishes
Users can submit a rating for individual dishes they have eaten. A rating is associated with the user and the dish, so it reflects their overall opinion of that dish rather than a single serving. A user rates a given dish once and can update that rating later.
Record how a serving compared to usual
Separate from the rating itself, every time a student eats a dish they can tap a button recording whether that serving was better than usual, as usual, or worse than usual. These accumulate into a running history, so the dish page can show how a dish has been lately without disturbing anyone's overall rating of it.
Rate a meal
Users can also rate a meal period overall, separate from any individual dish, giving a quick read on how lunch or dinner went as a whole.
View ratings and reviews
Users can see ratings and student feedback for dishes, along with how recent servings have compared to usual. This allows students to make more informed decisions about what to eat.
Submit comments and feedback
Users can leave comments about individual dishes or a dining hall meal. Users can also submit suggestions for dishes they would like Notre Dame Dining to serve.
Create and manage a user profile
Users can create an account and provide basic food preferences. They can also view and update their previous ratings and preferences.
View personal rating history
Users can see the dishes they have previously rated and their ratings over time. This information will also provide the foundation for the personalized recommendation functionality described in the advanced functions.
View dining information by date
Users can browse the menu for a specific date and meal period to see which dishes are being served, and read the ratings and comments on each one.

4.2 Advanced Functions

We plan to offer two advanced functions. Both go beyond the basic functions listed above, and each is technically challenging enough to require several days of design and implementation work on its own.

Advanced Function 1: Dish Recommendation System

A fully realized dish recommendation system based on a user's individual taste profile, built from their survey results and previous ratings.

What it does. Every user gets a ranked list of dishes they are likely to enjoy, surfaced directly on the menu view and in a separate suggested food tab. This is what closes the loop for the user: the more they rate, the better the suggestions get.

How it works

The engine blends three signals into a single score per candidate dish:

  • Survey-based content filtering. The optional sign-up survey and a dish's own attributes (cuisine type, protein, spice level, nutrition profile, dietary tags) produce an initial estimate for dishes the user has never rated. This is what makes recommendations possible before any rating history exists.
  • Dish-to-dish correlation. Once we have enough data, we build a correlation matrix between different dishes. If the people who loved dish A also consistently loved dish B, the two correlate, and a user's highest-rated dishes can be used to pull up correlated dishes they have not tried. Correlation is easier to work with than covariance because it is standardized, which should make the matrix more comparable across dishes with very different rating volumes.
  • Similar-user clustering. The Spotify-style signal: cluster users by their dish ratings and survey results, then suggest dishes that people in a similar cluster generally love, even if our user has never rated them.

Why it is technically challenging

  • Storing and maintaining the matrix. We need somewhere to keep the dish-by-dish matrix, and we cannot recompute it from scratch on every request. That means an incremental update strategy that folds in each new rating, which requires reasoning carefully about what happens to a pairwise statistic when a single user's rating changes.
  • Sparsity and cold start. With a few hundred users and thousands of dishes, the user-by-dish rating matrix is overwhelmingly empty. Naive correlation on sparse columns produces unstable, meaningless values, so we need minimum co-occurrence thresholds and smoothing.
  • Score blending and tuning. Combining survey, dish-to-dish, and similar-user scores into one ranking requires a weighting scheme, and we need a way to evaluate whether the recommendations are actually good.
  • Query cost. Producing a ranked list for one user across one day's menu has to run inside a page load, which forces real decisions about indexing, precomputation, and caching.

Why it is worth having

Recommendations are what make rating worth the effort. Without them, a student rates a dish and gets nothing back; with them, every rating measurably improves what the app shows them tomorrow. It is also the feature that most directly serves dining staff and students at once, since it converts scattered individual opinions into a clear signal about what is worth eating today.

Advanced Function 2: Comment Sections for Meals and Individual Dishes

Comment sections for the overall meal review and for individual dish reviews, with threaded replies, Giphy integration, and media upload.

What it does. Every dish and every meal period has a comment section. Students can reply to each other in nested threads, and comments can carry an uploaded photo of what they were actually served or a GIF from Giphy, which makes it easy for students to creatively express their opinions.

How it works

  • Threaded data model. Comments are stored with a self-referencing parent reference, and are attachable to either a dish or a meal period. Threads need to be fetched, nested, and paginated without loading an entire discussion tree on every page view.
  • Media upload pipeline. Users upload images which we validate (type, size), resize and thumbnail on the server, store on the filesystem, and reference by URL from the database, keeping binaries out of the database rows.
  • Third-party integration. Giphy is integrated through its API, which means handling API keys, rate limits, and the failure case where the upstream service is unavailable or slow.

Why it is technically challenging

Each of the three pieces above is a distinct engineering problem: recursive queries, binary file handling, and external API integration. A comment system that merely appends rows is a day's work; one that handles threaded rendering efficiently and media at scale, with a third-party dependency, is several.

Why it is worth having

Star ratings compress an opinion into a number and throw away everything interesting. Comments carry the specifics a student actually needs: that the pasta was cold, that the line was twenty minutes, that the vegan option was surprisingly good. That specificity is exactly what makes the feedback useful to dining staff. Media makes participation low-effort and genuinely fun: a photo of a plate says more than a paragraph, and it lowers the barrier enough that people actually contribute.

4.3 Future Extensions

Beyond the required scope, we are considering a "find a dining partner" feature that matches users with similar taste profiles, a dish-return notification system built on top of favorites, and eventually expanding Forkcast beyond the dining halls to other campus restaurants and beyond campuses.

5. ER Diagram & Assumptions

Our entity-relationship diagram covers the menu side (dining halls, menus, and the dishes on them) and the interaction side (students, their sign-up surveys, ratings, serving feedback, comments, and dish suggestions).

Assumptions

  1. Dish vs. menu appearance. A Dish is a canonical, persistent food item ("Chicken Tikka Masala"). A menu appearance is one serving of that dish at one dining hall, on one date, during one meal period, which we model as the relationship between a menu and a dish. Ratings and comments attach to the dish, not to any single appearance; only serving feedback carries a date.
  2. Ratings and comments attach to dishes, not servings. A rating is associated with a user and a dish, and persists rather than resetting each day. A user may rate any given dish at most once, enforced by a uniqueness constraint on (user, dish), and can update that rating later.
  3. Rating format. We are still deciding between a five-star scale, a like/dislike toggle, and three recommendation levels ("Don't recommend," "Recommend," "Strongly recommend"). Whichever we choose, the rating is a property of the (user, dish) pair and carries no date of its own.
  4. Serving feedback is recorded per visit. Day-to-day variation is captured by a separate entity rather than by the rating. Each time a user eats a dish they may record how that serving compared to usual (better than usual, as usual, or worse than usual), and those records are attached to the user and the dish and carry a date. A user may submit at most one such record per dish per day. We are still deciding exactly how to implement this, including how a record is tied to one particular serving and whether these three levels are the final set.
  5. Meals can be rated as a whole. Separate from individual dish ratings, a user may give an overall rating for a meal period at a dining hall, such as lunch at North Dining Hall on a given date. We model this on the meal period rather than on any single dish, and may also record how many users rated it.
  6. Dish name normalization. Scraped names are not guaranteed unique or consistently spelled across halls and dates. We assume a normalization step and an alias table mapping raw scraped names onto canonical dish records.
  7. Nutrition is one-to-one with a dish. Each canonical dish has exactly one nutrition record; nutrition is not stored per serving.
  8. Menu history is retained locally. We assume menus are persisted in our own database as they are scraped, so that past dates remain browsable.
  9. Users are authenticated. We assume each user has exactly one account and one profile, and that a user must be signed in to rate, comment, or vote.
  10. Some users are administrators. Student is modeled as a supertype with two subtypes, Regular User and Admin. Admins carry administrative privileges that regular users do not, which is what the Admin entity's admin_privileges attribute records. We use an ISA hierarchy rather than a single role attribute on Student because the two subtypes are distinguished by attributes of their own.
  11. The preference survey is optional. A user may have no survey response at all. In that case the recommendation engine falls back to popularity and collaborative signals rather than content-based ones. The survey is modeled as a set of declared preferences plus seed dish ratings.
  12. Comments are polymorphic but constrained. A comment attaches to either a dish (dish-level discussion) or a meal period (overall meal discussion). We model this as nullable foreign keys with a constraint enforcing that exactly one is set.
  13. Comments are threaded. A comment may reference a parent comment, forming a tree. Top-level comments have a null parent.
  14. Media is referenced, not stored inline. Images and GIFs belong to a comment. We assume binaries live on the server filesystem and the database stores only a URL and metadata; GIFs sourced from Giphy are referenced by their remote URL.
  15. Suggestions carry vote totals. A dish suggestion is a user-authored proposal. Upvote and downvote totals are stored on the suggestion record itself; the current design does not track which user cast which vote.
  16. Dining halls are seeded, not user-created. The set of Notre Dame dining locations is small and changes rarely, so dining hall records are inserted administratively rather than created through the application.
  17. The correlation matrix is derived data. The dish-by-dish correlation table is a cache computed from ratings, not a source of truth. It can be dropped and rebuilt from the ratings table at any time, which is what makes incremental updates safe.
  18. Favorites drive notifications. A user may favorite a dish; the system derives "your favorite is back" notifications by checking whether a favorited dish has a menu appearance on an upcoming date, rather than storing separate notification records per occurrence.
